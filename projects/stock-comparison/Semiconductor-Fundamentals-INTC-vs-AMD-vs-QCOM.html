I. Market Valuation & Growth

▾

Before analyzing any company, we must answer two foundational questions: how large is this business? and how fast is it expanding? The two primary measures—market capitalization and revenue growth—address these questions from different angles. Market cap reflects the collective judgment of all market participants about a company's total equity value, while revenue growth measures the rate at which the underlying business is scaling.

Understanding growth quantitatively requires a bit of care. A single year-over-year figure captures a snapshot, but the compound annual growth rate (CAGR) provides a smoothed, annualized measure of growth over multiple periods—removing the noise of any single year.

▾ Mathematical Framework

Market Capitalization is defined as:

\[ \text{Market Cap} = P \times N \]
where \( P \) is the current share price and \( N \) is the total number of shares outstanding.

Year-over-Year Growth Rate for any metric \( X \):

\[ g_{\text{YoY}} = \frac{X_t - X_{t-1}}{X_{t-1}} \times 100\% \]

Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) smooths growth over \( n \) years:

\[ \text{CAGR} = \left(\frac{X_n}{X_0}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}} - 1 \]
where \( X_0 \) is the starting value, \( X_n \) is the ending value, and \( n \) is the number of years. CAGR is the constant annual rate that would transform \( X_0 \) into \( X_n \) over \( n \) periods.

The Rule of 72 provides a quick mental shortcut: at a constant growth rate \( g \)% per year, a quantity approximately doubles in \( 72 / g \) years. This follows from solving \( 2 = (1 + g)^n \), which yields \( n = \ln 2 / \ln(1+g) \approx 72/g \) for small \( g \) (expressed as a percentage).

\[ n_{\text{double}} \approx \frac{72}{g\%} \]
For example, AMD's revenue growing at ~34% would imply a doubling time of roughly \( 72/34 \approx 2.1 \) years if sustained—an extraordinary pace.

II. The Profitability Hierarchy

▾

Profitability is not a single number—it is a layered structure. The income statement records a series of subtractions, each revealing a different dimension of how efficiently a company converts revenue into profit. Understanding this hierarchy is essential: two companies with identical revenue can have vastly different profitabilities depending on their cost structures, business models, and capital intensity.

The key idea is the profitability waterfall—starting from total revenue and progressively subtracting different categories of costs to arrive at increasingly refined measures of profit.

▾ Mathematical Framework

The Income Statement Waterfall:

Revenue (Sales)
− Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
= Gross Profit
− Operating Expenses (R&D + SG&A)
= Operating Income (EBIT)
− Interest Expense
− Taxes
= Net Income

Each level of profit defines a corresponding margin—the profit expressed as a percentage of revenue:

\[ \text{Gross Margin} = \frac{\text{Revenue} - \text{COGS}}{\text{Revenue}} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Revenue}} \]
\[ \text{Operating Margin} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Revenue}} \]
\[ \text{Net Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Revenue}} \]
The spread between margins reveals where costs are concentrated. For Intel, the gap between gross margin (35.43%) and operating margin (1.96%) is 33.47 percentage points—nearly all of its gross profit is consumed by operating expenses, primarily R&D ($16B+) and the cost of maintaining fabrication facilities.

Earnings Per Share (EPS) distributes net income to shareholders on a per-share basis:

\[ \text{EPS} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Weighted Average Shares Outstanding}} \]
Basic EPS uses actual shares outstanding. Diluted EPS includes the potential dilution from stock options, convertible bonds, and other instruments. Diluted EPS is always ≤ Basic EPS.

III. Cash Flow & Capital Allocation

▾

If the income statement tells you about accounting profitability, the cash flow statement tells you about economic reality. Accounting earnings can be distorted by non-cash charges (depreciation, amortization, stock-based compensation) and accrual timing. Cash flow strips away these artifacts and answers the critical question: how much actual cash did the business generate?

The most important metric in this section—and arguably in all of fundamental analysis—is Free Cash Flow (FCF). It is the cash left over after a company has funded both its operations and its capital investments. FCF is the cash available for dividends, share buybacks, debt reduction, and acquisitions. Warren Buffett has called it “owner earnings”—the true economic profit belonging to shareholders.

▾ Mathematical Framework

The Free Cash Flow Bridge reconciles net income to actual cash generation:

Net Income
+ Depreciation & Amortization (non-cash)
+ Stock-Based Compensation (non-cash)
± Changes in Working Capital
= Operating Cash Flow (OCF)
− Capital Expenditures (CapEx)
= Free Cash Flow (FCF)

Formally:

\[ \text{OCF} = \text{Net Income} + \text{D\&A} + \text{SBC} \pm \Delta\text{WC} \]
\[ \text{FCF} = \text{OCF} - \text{CapEx} \]
where \( \text{D\&A} \) = depreciation and amortization, \( \text{SBC} \) = stock-based compensation, and \( \Delta\text{WC} \) = change in working capital (increase in WC consumes cash; decrease in WC releases cash).

Capital Intensity Ratio measures how much of every revenue dollar goes to capital investment:

\[ \text{Capital Intensity} = \frac{\text{CapEx}}{\text{Revenue}} \]
Intel's ratio is \( \$13.10\text{B} / \$53.76\text{B} \approx 24.4\% \)—nearly a quarter of all revenue goes to building and upgrading fabs. Compare AMD at \( 2.8\% \) and QCOM at \( 4.0\% \), reflecting the capital-light fabless model.

FCF Yield relates free cash flow to market valuation:

\[ \text{FCF Yield} = \frac{\text{FCF}}{\text{Market Cap}} \times 100\% \]
FCF yield is the inverse of P/FCF. It can be interpreted as the cash return an investor earns on the current market price if all FCF were distributed. QCOM's FCF yield = \( \$12.50\text{B} / \$210.88\text{B} \approx 5.9\% \), which is attractive.

Why OCF can exceed Net Income: When a company reports a net loss but positive operating cash flow (as Intel does), it usually means large non-cash charges—particularly depreciation on expensive physical assets—are being added back. Intel's fabs depreciate by billions annually, which reduces reported earnings but does not consume cash.

High CapEx can indicate investment in future growth but reduces free cash flow. Capital-light (fabless) businesses have lower CapEx. Compare CapEx to revenue and depreciation for context.

IV. Valuation Multiples

▾

Valuation multiples are the most widely used tools in equity analysis. The core idea is simple: rather than building a full discounted cash flow model, we compare a company's market price to a fundamental measure of its business (earnings, sales, book value, or cash flow). This produces a ratio that can be compared across companies, industries, and time periods.

However, multiples are not just shortcuts—they are mathematically grounded in the same theory as DCF. A company's P/E ratio, for example, is not arbitrary: it can be algebraically derived from the Gordon Growth Model, and its value is determined by the company's growth rate, cost of equity, and payout ratio. Understanding this connection transforms multiples from blunt tools into precise instruments.

▾ Mathematical Framework

Equity vs. Enterprise Value: A critical distinction. Equity value (market cap) measures what shareholders own. Enterprise value (EV) measures the total value of the business to all capital providers—both equity and debt holders:

\[ \text{EV} = \text{Market Cap} + \text{Total Debt} - \text{Cash} \]
Equity multiples (P/E, P/B, P/S) compare equity value to equity-level measures. Enterprise multiples (EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales) compare total firm value to pre-debt measures. Mixing them (e.g., Market Cap / EBITDA) is a common error.

The P/E Ratio—Derived from Fundamentals:

Starting from the Gordon Growth Model for a stable-growth firm: \( P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r_e - g} \). Since dividends can be expressed as \( D_1 = \text{EPS}_1 \times \text{Payout Ratio} \), we can write:

\[ \frac{P_0}{\text{EPS}_1} = \frac{\text{Payout Ratio}}{r_e - g} \]
This reveals that P/E is a function of three variables: the payout ratio, the cost of equity \( r_e \), and the expected growth rate \( g \). A company deserves a higher P/E when it has higher growth, lower risk (lower \( r_e \)), or a higher payout ratio. This explains why AMD (high growth) trades at a higher P/E than QCOM (lower growth, higher payout).

Earnings Yield is simply the inverse of P/E:

\[ \text{Earnings Yield} = \frac{\text{EPS}}{P} = \frac{1}{\text{P/E}} \]
QCOM's earnings yield = \( 1/23.56 \approx 4.24\% \). This can be compared directly to bond yields to gauge relative attractiveness of stocks versus bonds.

EV/EBITDA is preferred by many analysts because:

\[ \frac{\text{EV}}{\text{EBITDA}} = \frac{\text{Market Cap} + \text{Debt} - \text{Cash}}{\text{EBIT} + \text{D\&A}} \]
It accounts for differences in capital structure (debt levels), tax regimes, and depreciation policies across companies. EBITDA approximates pre-tax, pre-financing operating cash flow, making it useful for cross-company comparisons regardless of how each company is financed.

V. Capital Efficiency & Returns

▾

Profitability tells you how much money a company makes. Return metrics tell you how efficiently it deploys capital to generate that profit. A company earning $1B looks impressive until you realize it required $100B in assets to do so (1% return). Return metrics normalize profit by the capital base, answering: for each dollar of capital entrusted to management, how many cents of profit were generated?

This distinction is economically fundamental. A company creates value only when its return on capital exceeds its cost of capital. If a firm earns 6% on its invested capital but its investors demand 10%, the company is destroying shareholder value with every dollar it reinvests.

▾ Mathematical Framework

Return on Assets (ROA) measures how efficiently all assets generate profit:

\[ \text{ROA} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Total Assets}} \times 100\% \]
Asset-heavy companies (like Intel with fabs) naturally have lower ROA. Compare within the same business model: fabless vs. IDM (integrated device manufacturer) companies are structurally different.

Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) is more precise—it measures the return on capital that was deliberately invested in the business:

\[ \text{ROIC} = \frac{\text{NOPAT}}{\text{Invested Capital}} = \frac{\text{EBIT} \times (1-t)}{\text{Equity} + \text{Debt} - \text{Cash}} \]
where \( \text{NOPAT} \) = Net Operating Profit After Tax = \( \text{EBIT} \times (1 - t) \), and \( t \) is the effective tax rate. ROIC uses after-tax operating income (before interest expense) divided by all invested capital, making it independent of capital structure.

The Value Creation Test: A company creates economic value if and only if:

\[ \text{ROIC} > \text{WACC} \]
The spread \( (\text{ROIC} - \text{WACC}) \) multiplied by invested capital gives Economic Value Added (EVA), a measure of dollar-value creation: \( \text{EVA} = (\text{ROIC} - \text{WACC}) \times \text{Invested Capital} \).

Dividend Yield measures the cash income return from holding a stock:

\[ \text{Dividend Yield} = \frac{\text{Annual Dividends Per Share}}{\text{Share Price}} \times 100\% \]
Total shareholder return combines dividend yield with capital gains: \( \text{TSR} = \text{Dividend Yield} + \text{Capital Gain Yield} \). A company paying no dividend (like AMD) must deliver all returns through price appreciation.

VI. DuPont Analysis

▾

The DuPont decomposition—developed by the DuPont Corporation in the 1920s—is one of the most elegant tools in financial analysis. It answers a deceptively simple question: why does one company earn a higher return on equity than another? Rather than treating ROE as a single opaque number, DuPont breaks it into its component drivers, revealing whether a company's returns come from profitability, efficiency, or leverage.

This decomposition is not just an accounting identity—it is a diagnostic framework. By isolating the three levers management can pull, it identifies where a company excels and where it is weak.

▾ Mathematical Framework

Return on Equity (ROE) is the foundational metric:

\[ \text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}} \]

3-Factor DuPont Decomposition: We can decompose ROE by strategically multiplying by 1 (in the form of Revenue/Revenue and Assets/Assets):

\[ \text{ROE} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Revenue}}}_{\text{Profit Margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Total Assets}}}_{\text{Asset Turnover}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Total Assets}}{\text{Equity}}}_{\text{Equity Multiplier}} \]
Derivation. Start with ROE = Net Income / Equity. Multiply by \( \frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Revenue}} \times \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Assets}} = 1 \):

\( \displaystyle \text{ROE} = \frac{\text{NI}}{\text{Equity}} \times \frac{\text{Rev}}{\text{Rev}} \times \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Assets}} = \frac{\text{NI}}{\text{Rev}} \times \frac{\text{Rev}}{\text{Assets}} \times \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Equity}} \)

Each factor captures a distinct strategic dimension:

1. Profit Margin = Net Income / Revenue — How much profit per dollar of sales? Reflects pricing power, cost control, and operational efficiency.

2. Asset Turnover = Revenue / Total Assets — How efficiently are assets deployed to generate sales? Capital-light businesses (fabless semiconductors) have high turnover.

3. Equity Multiplier = Total Assets / Equity — How much leverage is used? Higher multiplier = more debt. A multiplier of 2.0 means 50% of assets are debt-financed.

5-Factor DuPont Decomposition: For deeper analysis, the profit margin can be further decomposed into tax burden and interest burden components:

\[ \text{ROE} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{NI}}{\text{EBT}}}_{\text{Tax Burden}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{EBT}}{\text{EBIT}}}_{\text{Interest Burden}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Rev}}}_{\text{Operating Margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Rev}}{\text{Assets}}}_{\text{Turnover}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Equity}}}_{\text{Leverage}} \]
where EBT = Earnings Before Tax. The tax burden ratio \( (1 - t_{\text{eff}}) \) measures the fraction of pre-tax profit retained after taxes. The interest burden ratio measures how much operating profit survives after interest payments.

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)

Valuing a Company by Its Future Cash — From First Principles

◆ The Time Value of Money

The entire edifice of DCF rests on a single axiom: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. This is not merely a statement about inflation—it reflects the fundamental economic fact that money received today can be invested to generate returns. This concept, called the time value of money, is the bedrock of all modern finance.

If you can earn a return \( r \) per period, then a cash flow \( C \) received \( t \) periods from now has a present value of:

\[ PV = \frac{C}{(1 + r)^t} \]

The factor \( \frac{1}{(1+r)^t} \) is the discount factor—it shrinks future cash flows to reflect the opportunity cost of waiting. Conversely, the future value of \( C \) invested today for \( t \) periods is:

\[ FV = C \times (1 + r)^t \]

◆ The DCF Framework

A DCF valuation estimates a company's intrinsic value by projecting its future free cash flows and discounting them to the present. The total value is the sum of two components: the explicit forecast period (typically 5–10 years) and the terminal value (the value of all cash flows beyond the forecast horizon).

\[ V_0 = \underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{FCF_t}{(1+r)^t}}_{\text{Present value of forecast period}} + \underbrace{\frac{TV_n}{(1+r)^n}}_{\text{Present value of terminal value}} \]
where \( FCF_t \) = free cash flow in year \( t \), \( r \) = discount rate (typically WACC), \( n \) = number of projection years, and \( TV_n \) = terminal value at the end of year \( n \).

◆ Cost of Equity: The CAPM

The Capital Asset Pricing Model (Sharpe, 1964; Lintner, 1965) provides a systematic way to estimate the return that equity investors require—the cost of equity \( r_e \). The key insight is that investors are only compensated for systematic risk (market risk that cannot be diversified away), measured by beta (\( \beta \)).

\[ r_e = r_f + \beta \cdot (r_m - r_f) \]
Derivation sketch: In equilibrium, CAPM says the expected return on any asset \( i \) must compensate for:
• \( r_f \) = the risk-free rate (typically the 10-year Treasury yield, ~4.2% currently)
• \( \beta_i \) = the asset's sensitivity to market movements, defined as \( \beta_i = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\text{Var}(R_m)} \)
• \( (r_m - r_f) \) = the equity risk premium (ERP)—the excess return investors demand for bearing market risk (~5–6% historically)

For a semiconductor company like QCOM with \( \beta \approx 1.2 \):

\[ r_e = 4.2\% + 1.2 \times 5.5\% = 4.2\% + 6.6\% = 10.8\% \]
Note on CAPM limitations: While widely used, CAPM has known shortcomings. Fama & French (1993) showed that size and value factors also explain returns beyond beta alone. The model assumes investors hold perfectly diversified portfolios—in practice, many do not. Despite these criticisms, CAPM remains the standard starting point for estimating cost of equity in industry practice.

◆ Cost of Debt

The cost of debt \( r_d \) is the interest rate a company pays on its borrowings. Because interest expense is tax-deductible, the after-tax cost of debt is lower than the stated rate:

\[ r_d^{\text{after-tax}} = r_d \times (1 - t) \]
where \( t \) is the marginal corporate tax rate. This tax shield is one reason companies use debt financing—it effectively subsidizes borrowing. If QCOM borrows at 4.5% and faces a 21% tax rate: \( r_d^{\text{after-tax}} = 4.5\% \times (1 - 0.21) = 3.56\% \).

◆ Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

WACC blends the cost of equity and the after-tax cost of debt, weighted by their proportion of the company's total capital structure. It represents the overall rate of return that the company must earn on its investments to satisfy all capital providers.

\[ \text{WACC} = \frac{E}{V} \cdot r_e + \frac{D}{V} \cdot r_d \cdot (1 - t) \]
where:
• \( E \) = market value of equity, \( D \) = market value of debt, \( V = E + D \)
• \( r_e \) = cost of equity (from CAPM), \( r_d \) = cost of debt
• \( t \) = corporate tax rate

Worked example for QCOM:

\[ \text{WACC} = \frac{210.88}{210.88 + 15.60} \times 10.8\% + \frac{15.60}{226.48} \times 4.5\% \times (1 - 0.21) \]
\[ = 0.931 \times 10.8\% + 0.069 \times 3.56\% = 10.05\% + 0.25\% \approx 10.3\% \]
QCOM's capital structure is heavily equity-weighted, so WACC is close to the cost of equity. We round to 10% for the DCF example below.

◆ Terminal Value: The Gordon Growth Model

Since we cannot project cash flows forever, we compute a terminal value at the end of the forecast period. The most common approach assumes cash flows grow at a constant rate \( g \) in perpetuity. This is the Gordon Growth Model (Gordon & Shapiro, 1956).

Derivation from a geometric series:

The terminal value at time \( n \) is the sum of all future discounted cash flows growing at rate \( g \):
\[ TV_n = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{FCF_n \cdot (1+g)^k}{(1+r)^k} = FCF_n \cdot (1+g) \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^k \]
This is an infinite geometric series with ratio \( \frac{1+g}{1+r} \). Since \( g < r \), the ratio is less than 1 and the series converges:
\[ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} x^k = \frac{1}{1-x} \quad \text{for } |x| < 1 \]
Substituting \( x = \frac{1+g}{1+r} \):
\[ TV_n = FCF_n \cdot (1+g) \cdot \frac{1}{1 - \frac{1+g}{1+r}} = FCF_n \cdot (1+g) \cdot \frac{1+r}{r-g} \]
Which simplifies to the standard form (since the \( (1+r) \) cancels when we discount back):
\[ \boxed{TV_n = \frac{FCF_n \cdot (1+g)}{r - g}} \]
Critical constraint: \( g \) must be less than \( r \); otherwise the series diverges. The terminal growth rate should not exceed the long-term GDP growth rate (~2–3%), since no company can grow faster than the economy indefinitely.

◆ Counter-Example: Why DCF Fails for INTC

INTC's FCF is −$3.12B. You cannot project negative FCF forward in a standard DCF and get a meaningful valuation. DCF requires positive, predictable cash flows. For turnaround stories like Intel, analysts must estimate when FCF turns positive and use scenario analysis—building multiple DCF models under different assumptions (optimistic, base, pessimistic) and probability-weighting the outcomes.

◆ Key Limitations

  • Highly sensitive to assumptions—as the sensitivity table shows, small changes in WACC or growth rate dramatically change the result
  • Garbage in, garbage out—projections are educated guesses. Aswath Damodaran notes: “A DCF valuation is only as good as the story that underlies it”
  • Terminal value often accounts for 60–80% of total value, making the most uncertain component also the most influential
  • Works best for mature, stable cash-flow-generating businesses (like QCOM). Less useful for high-growth (AMD) or turnaround (INTC) companies
  • CAPM's beta is estimated from historical data, which may not predict future risk. Fama & French (1992, 1993) showed additional risk factors beyond market beta
  • The perpetuity assumption in terminal value implies the company exists forever—a strong assumption for any individual firm

Glossary of Terms

Quick reference for financial terms used throughout this page
Beta (β)A stock's sensitivity to market movements. β = 1 means it moves with the market; β > 1 means more volatile.
Book ValueTotal assets minus total liabilities. The accounting (net asset) value of shareholders' equity.
CAGRCompound Annual Growth Rate. The smoothed annualized return over multiple periods.
CAPMCapital Asset Pricing Model. Relates expected return to systematic risk via beta. See VII.
CapExCapital Expenditure. Cash spent on physical assets (property, plant, equipment).
COGSCost of Goods Sold. Direct costs of producing goods or services (materials, manufacturing, labor).
DCFDiscounted Cash Flow. Valuation method based on projecting and discounting future free cash flows. See VII.
D&ADepreciation & Amortization. Non-cash charges that allocate asset costs over their useful life.
DuPontA decomposition of ROE into profit margin, asset turnover, and leverage. See VI.
EBITEarnings Before Interest and Taxes. Same as operating income. Measures core profitability.
EBITDAEBIT plus depreciation and amortization. Approximates pre-tax operating cash flow.
EPSEarnings Per Share. Net income divided by shares outstanding.
ERPEquity Risk Premium. The extra return investors demand for stocks over risk-free bonds (~5–6%).
EVEnterprise Value. Market cap + debt − cash. Total value of the firm to all capital providers.
EVAEconomic Value Added. (ROIC − WACC) × Invested Capital. Dollar measure of value creation.
FablessA semiconductor company that designs chips but outsources manufacturing (e.g., AMD, QCOM use TSMC).
FCFFree Cash Flow. Operating cash flow minus capital expenditures. Cash available for shareholders.
IDMIntegrated Device Manufacturer. A company that both designs and fabricates chips (e.g., Intel).
Market CapShare price × shares outstanding. The total market value of a company's equity.
NOPATNet Operating Profit After Tax. EBIT × (1 − tax rate). Used in ROIC calculation.
OCFOperating Cash Flow. Cash generated from core business operations, before CapEx.
P/EPrice-to-Earnings ratio. Share price divided by EPS. The most common valuation multiple.
P/BPrice-to-Book ratio. Market cap divided by book value of equity.
P/SPrice-to-Sales ratio. Market cap divided by revenue.
ROAReturn on Assets. Net income / total assets. Measures asset utilization efficiency.
ROEReturn on Equity. Net income / shareholders' equity. Key metric in DuPont analysis.
ROICReturn on Invested Capital. NOPAT / invested capital. Measures capital efficiency. See V.
SBCStock-Based Compensation. Non-cash expense recognized when employees receive equity awards.
SG&ASelling, General & Administrative expenses. Overhead costs not directly tied to production.
TTMTrailing Twelve Months. Financial data summing the most recent four quarters.
WACCWeighted Average Cost of Capital. Blended cost of debt and equity. Used as DCF discount rate. See VII.
