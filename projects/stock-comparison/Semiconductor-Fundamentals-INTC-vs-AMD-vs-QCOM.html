Market capitalization is the total market value of a company's outstanding shares. It represents what the market collectively believes the entire company is worth at the current share price.
▾ Formula
Market Cap = Share Price × Shares Outstanding

Revenue is the total income generated from selling goods or services before any expenses are deducted. It is often called the “top line” because it sits at the top of the income statement.

Revenue = Units Sold × Average Price (simplified)

Higher revenue indicates larger business scale. But revenue alone tells you nothing about profitability — a company can have massive revenue and still lose money if costs exceed income.

Gross profit is revenue minus the direct cost of producing goods or services (COGS). It represents the profit available before operating expenses like R&D and overhead.
▾ Formula
Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

Higher gross profit means more money available to cover operating expenses, fund R&D, and generate bottom-line profit. Always compare alongside gross margin to understand efficiency.

▾ What is it?
Gross margin is the percentage of revenue retained after subtracting direct production costs. It measures production efficiency and pricing power.
▾ Formula
Gross Margin = (Gross Profit / Revenue) × 100
▾ How to Interpret
Higher margins indicate better pricing power or lower production costs. In semiconductors, fabless companies (AMD, QCOM) typically have higher margins than those running their own fabs (INTC). Above 50% is considered strong.
▾ By the Numbers
QCOM leads at 54.80%, AMD at 49.52%, INTC at 35.43%. Intel's lower margin reflects the high cost of owning fabrication facilities. QCOM's licensing (QTL) segment contributes very high-margin revenue.

Operating income (also called EBIT) is the profit remaining after subtracting operating expenses (R&D, SG&A) from gross profit. It measures core business profitability before interest and taxes.

Operating Income = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses (R&D + SG&A + Other)

Positive operating income means the core business is profitable. A company with high revenue but low or negative operating income has a cost structure problem.
▾ By the Numbers

Cash Flow

▾
Metric INTC AMD QCOM
Operating Cash Flow INTC$9.98B AMD$7.71B QCOM$14.29B
▾ What is it?
Operating cash flow (OCF) is the cash generated from core business operations. Unlike net income, it excludes non-cash accounting items like depreciation and reflects actual cash movement.
▾ Formula
OCF = Net Income + Non-Cash Charges (Depreciation, Amortization) ± Changes in Working Capital
▾ How to Interpret
Positive and growing OCF is essential — it funds CapEx, dividends, buybacks, and debt repayment. When OCF exceeds net income, it generally indicates high-quality earnings.
▾ By the Numbers
QCOM leads at $14.29B. INTC generates $9.98B — positive despite a net loss, because large depreciation add-backs from fab assets restore cash. AMD at $7.71B.
Capital Expenditure INTC-$13.10B AMD-$974M QCOM-$1.78B
▾ What is it?
Capital expenditure (CapEx) is money spent on acquiring, maintaining, or upgrading physical assets — factories, equipment, technology. Shown as negative because it is a cash outflow.
▾ Formula
CapEx = Purchases of Property, Plant & Equipment (from cash flow statement)
▾ How to Interpret
High CapEx can indicate investment in future growth but reduces free cash flow. Capital-light (fabless) businesses have lower CapEx. Compare CapEx to revenue and depreciation for context.
▾ By the Numbers
INTC’s −$13.10B is massive — nearly 24% of revenue goes to building and upgrading fabs. AMD at −$974M and QCOM at −$1.78B are far lower as fabless companies outsourcing to TSMC.
Free Cash Flow INTC-$3.12B AMD$6.74B QCOM$12.50B
▾ What is it?
Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left over after a company has paid for operations and capital expenditures. It is the cash available for dividends, buybacks, debt reduction, or acquisitions.
▾ Formula
FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures
▾ How to Interpret
Positive FCF is critical for financial health and shareholder returns. Negative FCF means the company is burning cash. Consistently high FCF is one of the most important indicators of a quality business.
▾ By the Numbers
QCOM leads at $12.50B — enormous cash generation. AMD at $6.74B benefits from its fabless model. INTC’s −$3.12B is deeply negative because massive CapEx ($13.10B) exceeds operating cash flow ($9.98B).

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)

Valuing a Company by Its Future Cash

◆ What is DCF?

DCF estimates a company's intrinsic value by projecting its future free cash flows and discounting them back to today's dollars. The core idea: a dollar earned in the future is worth less than a dollar today, because of the time value of money.

◆ The Formula

Intrinsic Value = Σ [ FCFt / (1 + r)t ] + Terminal Value / (1 + r)n

Where:
  FCFt = Free Cash Flow in year t
  r = Discount rate (typically WACC, ~8–12%)
  n = Projection period (typically 5–10 years)
  Terminal Value = FCFn × (1 + g) / (r − g), where g = long-term growth rate

◆ Counter-Example: Why DCF Fails for INTC

INTC's FCF is −$3.12B. You cannot project negative FCF forward and get a meaningful valuation. DCF requires positive, predictable cash flows. For turnaround stories like Intel, analysts must estimate when FCF turns positive and use scenario analysis instead.

◆ Key Limitations

  • Highly sensitive to assumptions — small changes in growth rate or WACC dramatically change the result
  • Garbage in, garbage out — projections are educated guesses at best
  • Terminal value often accounts for 60–80% of total value, making it the most uncertain component
  • Works best for mature, stable cash-flow-generating businesses (like QCOM)
  • Less useful for high-growth (AMD) or turnaround (INTC) companies
